# Research: prior art — scripted-bot battle games and user-programmable worlds

- **Date:** 2026-11-08
- **Status:** research deliverable for [#5988](https://github.com/developing-today/code/issues/5988); docs-only, no implementation is included here.
- **Issue:** [#5988](https://github.com/developing-today/code/issues/5988), part of the worlds design program [#5955](https://github.com/developing-today/code/issues/5955) (statements S47, S107, S109 of the 2026-11-04 brief).
- **Deliverable shape (per #5988):** per-system entries in lineage A with fixed fields — _user submits X; bounded by Y; capabilities granted via Z; sharing model; moderation model; what broke_ — one pass each over lineage B against four themes (user-code resource limits, code sharing/reuse as libraries, moderation of user creations, asset licensing), then a synthesis table mapping predecessor mechanisms to the #5955 design children that borrow them: the per-user mapping child, the server-invariants child ([#5962](https://github.com/developing-today/code/issues/5962)), and the governance child.
- **Method and honesty notes:** entries are written from the public historical and documentary record of each system (manuals, post-mortems, platform documentation, well-known incident write-ups). Two naming notes matter. (1) **"Crowbots"** in #5988 is taken to be **CROBOTS** (Tom Poindexter, 1985) — the documented system whose name matches ("see-robots"); no separate system published as "Crowbots" could be found in the record, and the entry below says so rather than inventing one. (2) **"Botland / Botlands"** is taken to be **Bot Land** (bot.land, Adam Damiano, launched 2019). Where a number is version-dependent (limits changed across eras), the entry gives the era it describes instead of a false single figure.

## Why this research exists

The #5955 worlds program lets users submit code that a server then runs for them, persistently, next to other users' code. That is not a new invention; it is a forty-year-old one with two intertwined family trees. Lineage A (battle games) explored the _economics of bounded execution_ — how much computation a submitted program may spend, and what happens when the bound is wrong. Lineage B (programmable worlds) explored the _politics of persistent user code_ — capabilities, sharing, licensing, and what a community does when someone's creation harms someone else. The design children of #5955 inherit both sets of problems at once, which is why this doc covers both lineages and ends in a mapping, not just a survey.

## Lineage A — scripted-bot battle games

### A1. Core War (1984)

- **User submits:** a "warrior" — a short program in Redcode, a minimal assembly language for the MARS virtual machine (Memory Array Redcode Simulator). Warriors are loaded into a circular core memory at random offsets and executed in round-robin interleaving; a warrior wins by causing every opponent process to execute an illegal instruction (typically a `DAT`).
- **Bounded by:** the core itself (fixed memory size, standardized at 8,000 cells under the ICWS'88 standard, with a maximum warrior length of 100 instructions) and by tournament-imposed execution bounds — a maximum cycle count per battle (commonly 80,000 cycles in modern hills) after which survivors tie, and limits on processes per warrior. The bound is _per program, symmetric, and total_: every warrior gets the same machine, and the machine has no outside. This is the gas model of the whole lineage, forty years early: a fixed instruction budget, metered by the referee, identical for every submitter.
- **Capabilities granted via:** nothing but core memory operations. There is no API, no I/O, no syscall surface — a warrior's entire capability set is reading, writing, copying, and jumping within the shared core, including over its opponents' code. Self-modification is not an exploit; it is the medium.
- **Sharing model:** radically open. Winning warriors were published in full — in A. K. Dewdney's _Scientific American_ "Computer Recreations" columns (1984 onward), in newsletters, and later on King-of-the-Hill servers (notably the KOTH.org hills) where submitted warriors' source was visible and the reigning hill entries were there to be studied and beaten. Strategy diffused by reading the champion's code.
- **Moderation model:** almost none needed, and that is itself the lesson: the arena is hermetic, matches are stateless, and a warrior can only harm other warriors inside a bounded match. Governance reduced to tournament/hill rules (standard settings, entry limits per author on some hills) administered by hill operators.
- **What broke:** arms-race dynamics rather than safety. Self-replicating and self-repairing warriors, "vampires" that hijack opponent processes, evolver-written warriors (programs bred by genetic algorithms) that no human could read, and a mature rock-paper-scissors meta (papers, scanners, bombers, imps) in which dominance is cyclic, not progressive. Draws are common enough that tie-handling is a first-class rule. The failure mode to import: _when execution is free-form inside the arena, the meta becomes adversarial program evolution_ — expect users' worlds code to be optimized against the metering rules themselves, not just against other users.

### A2. Robot War (1981) and the robot-programming line

- **User submits:** a robot program in Robot War's purpose-built interpreted language (Silas Warner, Muse Software, Apple II, 1981) — a small language with built-in variables bound to the robot's hardware registers (radar, range, speed, damage, energy). Up to five robots share an arena; the last robot functioning wins.
- **Bounded by:** the physics/economics of the simulation rather than an instruction counter: robots have finite energy and take damage; the interpreter runs all robots in interleaved steps so no robot can monopolize the (single, physical) CPU for long. Later descendants made the bound explicit and per-program — the direct ancestors of per-tick budgets:
  - **RoboWar** (David Harris, Macintosh, 1990s): robots were distributed as compiled, _password-protected_ files — the author could lock a robot so opponents could not read its code — with a chronon-based time unit metered per robot.
  - **Robocode** (Mat Nelson, 2000; later open-sourced and community-maintained): robots are Java (later also .NET) classes run under a security manager in a sandbox; the engine measures each robot's CPU time per turn, and a robot that consistently overruns gets its turns _skipped_, then disabled — metering enforced as in-game consequence, not rejection.
  - **Battlecode** (MIT's annual tournament, 2000s–present): teams submit Java (in recent years also other-language) players for a full RTS match; the bound is an explicit _bytecode budget per robot per turn_ (historically on the order of 10,000 bytecodes per turn, with bytecode carry-over banking in some years) — the clearest "gas with banking" design in the lineage.
  - **Halite / Kaggle simulation competitions** (2016–2020): bots in any language run as separate processes against per-turn wall-clock limits on the referee's machine; overruns forfeit moves or the match. The bound moved from in-fiction metering to referee wall-clock, because the referee no longer controls the language runtime.
- **Capabilities granted via:** a fixed sensor/actuator surface — radar/range queries, movement, and a weapon — exposed as language built-ins (Robot War), an API library (Robocode's `Robot`/`AdvancedRobot` classes), or a game API over the match state (Battlecode, Halite). No robot in the line ever gets general-purpose I/O during a match; capability = the documented game API, nothing else.
- **Sharing model:** split down the middle, and the split is instructive. Core War published everything; the robot line often did the opposite — RoboWar robots were deliberately opaque (password-locked) so a champion's strategy stayed secret, and Robocode's community norms mixed open-source teaching robots with closed competition entries. Battlecode and Halite/Kaggle went back to open: winning teams' code and post-mortems are routinely published after the season, which is where most of the learning in those communities lives.
- **Moderation model:** tournament administration — version pinning of the engine, disqualification for sandbox escapes or referee exploits, and (in Robocode's RoboRumble) community-run continuous ladders with entry rules. Because matches are hermetic and ephemeral like Core War's, moderation never had to become community governance.
- **What broke:** referee and sandbox exploits are the recurring incident class — Robocode's history includes robots that attacked the sandbox/referee rather than the opponent (hence the security-manager hardening and the rule that exploiting the engine is disqualifying, not clever); Battlecode seasons repeatedly discovered that the cheapest winning strategy was an unintended degenerate one (mass cheap units, pathing exploits), forcing mid-lineage rules and map changes; and per-turn budgets taught a durable lesson — _any_ meter (bytecodes, wall-clock, chronons) becomes the real game board: top players optimize against the meter's exact accounting (e.g., banking bytecodes, splitting work across turns and across units) rather than against the nominal strategy space.

### A3. CROBOTS — the system named "Crowbots" in #5988 (1985)

- **Identification:** Tom Poindexter's CROBOTS ("see-robots"), first released December 1985 as shareware for MS-DOS, open-sourced under GPLv2 in 2013. No distinct system published under the spelling "Crowbots" appears in the record; CROBOTS is the robot-game of this era whose name matches, and it sits in the Robot War line (it credits RobotWar and Core War as inspirations). This entry covers CROBOTS under the issue's name for it.
- **User submits:** a robot program written in a stripped-down subset of C (integers, functions, `if`/`while`; no pointers, arrays, floats, or recursion), written in a text editor and compiled by the game's own C compiler into its virtual machine. Up to four robots compete per match on a 1,000 × 1,000 metre battlefield.
- **Bounded by:** the virtual machine and the match, not an explicit fuel counter: the language subset is small enough to be safe and slow enough to be fair, all robots run on the same VM in real time, and matches end on destruction or elapsed limits in match play. The bound here is _expressiveness_ — the language is the sandbox: remove pointers, memory access, and I/O, and a submitted program cannot reach anything outside its robot.
- **Capabilities granted via:** intrinsic "hardware" functions linked into the VM — `scan()` (find opponents by bearing/range), `cannon()` (fire, range 700 m, limited missiles in flight), `drive()` (set heading/speed), damage/speed/location queries. The intrinsic library is the complete capability surface, callable for free (intrinsics consume no program space) — an early, clean example of _capabilities as a function library over a sealed language_.
- **Sharing model:** source-level and communal: sample robots shipped with the game (Rabbit, Counter, Rook, Sniper) as teaching code of increasing sophistication; robots circulated as `.r` source files among players and BBS/Usenet communities; the 2013 GPLv2 release made the engine itself shared infrastructure, and clones (C++Robots by email, Netrobots, Crobots64) reused the design openly.
- **Moderation model:** none beyond the local match — CROBOTS is a take-turns local program, not a service. Nothing a robot does persists or reaches another user's machine beyond a shared match on one computer, so there was nothing to moderate; tournaments in the descendant C++Robots (play-by-email, with a human-run server and a reigning "hill") added entry handling by the operator.
- **What broke:** very little, _because_ the surface was so small — which is the finding. The costs of the sealed-language bound showed up as ceiling effects instead: no data structures worth the name, strategies capped at what a few hundred lines of pointer-less C can express, and the genre's centre of gravity moved to richer hosts (Java for Robocode, JS for Screeps) that traded CROBOTS' safety-by-language for capability — and then had to rebuild the bounds as metering and sandboxing instead.

### A4. Gladiabots (2019)

- **User submits:** not code but _visual logic_ — behaviour trees built from action and condition nodes (move to / flee from / attack the weakest enemy, etc.) in an in-game graphical editor, assigned per robot class in a squad. Gladiabots (GFX47, Steam/mobile, 2019) is the modern proof that the submission in this lineage need not be text at all: the artifact is a data structure the engine interprets node by node.
- **Bounded by:** engine evaluation order and tick rules — each robot's tree is evaluated against the current world state at the game's decision cadence, node evaluation is deterministic and cheap by construction (a tree walk, not a program), and balance bounds do the rest: fixed squad sizes, per-class stats, match timers, and mode-specific objectives (elimination, domination, collection). There is no resource a player can overspend; the bound is the _node vocabulary and evaluation semantics_.
- **Capabilities granted via:** the node vocabulary itself — the set of conditions the engine will evaluate (enemy filters by health/shield/distance/class, ally and objective states) and the set of actions it will execute. Granting a capability means adding a node type; there is no escape hatch and no user-defined function, so the capability surface is fully enumerable by the designer.
- **Sharing model:** community diffusion rather than code import: players publish and discuss tree designs (forums, Discord, wiki), and the game supports deploying your AIs against other players' squads asynchronously — you fight recorded/deploy-time snapshots of opponents' logic, so the meta is visible and copyable _in effect_ (you can watch exactly what a champion tree does in replays) even where it is not importable as a file.
- **Moderation model:** minimal by architecture. Competition is asynchronous against deployed AI snapshots — there is no live opponent to harass, no chat surface of consequence in-match, and no persistent world for a bad artifact to poison. Ladder integrity (deployment rules, league placement) is the whole of governance.
- **What broke:** expressiveness ceiling and meta-collapse, the visual-logic versions of A1/A3's problems: a small number of "universal" tree architectures turned out to dominate across classes, balance became a patch treadmill on node semantics and class stats, and veteran players hit the ceiling of what trees can express (no memory abstractions beyond tags/counters, awkward state machines) — the community's skill expression compressed into micro-optimizing evaluation-order quirks. Lesson for #5955: _a no-code submission format buys safety and approachability, and pays for it with a hard design ceiling and a meta that converges fast_; it is a tier, not a substitute, for real code.

### A5. Botland / Bot Land (2019)

- **User submits:** bot scripts at one of three tiers, player's choice per bot: (1) no script — a built-in default AI; (2) **Blockly** — Scratch-like visual blocks that compile to JavaScript under the hood; (3) **BotLandScript** — a text language that is a deliberate _subset of JavaScript_, input directly in the game's editor. Bot Land (Adam Damiano; developed ~2015–2019, launched 2019, free-to-play on web/desktop/mobile) is the bridge system of lineage A: CROBOTS' sealed-subset idea, rebuilt for the web, with a visual on-ramp.
- **Bounded by:** the language subset and the simulation: BotLandScript restricts JavaScript severely (member expressions, for example, are allowed only in extremely narrow cases; only whitelisted functions exist), bots are fully automated and run by the server/referee in matches the player does not control live, and equipment/ability rules bound what even a perfect script can do (some items cannot be used by the default AI at all). As in CROBOTS, the primary bound is _what the submitted language can say_, backed by server-side execution.
- **Bounded by (economics):** a free-to-play game's economy sits outside the match sandbox — progression and items gate power, which deliberately decouples "best script" from "wins everything": scripting skill is one input among several, softening the pure-code arms race of A1–A3.
- **Capabilities granted via:** the documented function set exposed in the script editor (the editor's help enumerates every callable) — sensors over entities (`entity.life`-style reads in the narrow allowed forms), movement/attack/item actions, and `Math` helpers. If a function is not in the list, it does not exist for a bot.
- **Sharing model:** snippet-first: the game ships premade scripts and snippets for the block tier so non-programmers start from working parts, and community sharing of scripts/strategies happens through the game's community channels; the block tier's scripts are inspectable as blocks, lowering the copy-and-learn barrier that RoboWar deliberately raised.
- **Moderation model:** service-standard (accounts, reports, developer-run servers) but, as in Gladiabots, the abuse surface is small by construction: matches are automated and effectively asynchronous, scripts are sealed data run by the referee, and there is no persistent shared world state for a script to vandalize.
- **What broke:** the two-audience problem, in the open: the developer's own materials concede Blockly and BotLandScript only _aspire_ to feature parity, so the visual tier is a dialect that can strand its users; the default AI vs. scripted-bot gap makes unscripted play a tutorial, not a peer; and a solo-developer service could not out-patch the meta — the recurring lineage-A lesson that _the referee is a live product_, not a one-time artifact. For #5955, Bot Land is the worked example of tiered submission (none / visual / text-subset) over one capability surface.

### A6. Screeps (2015– )

- **User submits:** JavaScript (also WebAssembly modules, and other languages compiled to WASM) that runs _continuously and persistently_ on the game's servers — not per match but as a living colony in a shared MMO world. Screeps (Screeps Team, Steam/Early Access 2015, full release 2016) is the closest living relative of the whole #5955 program: user code _is_ the player's presence, running 24/7 whether or not the player is online. Code is organized in modules with a `loop` entry point, executed once per game tick in an isolated VM per player.
- **Bounded by:** the most explicit resource economics in this entire survey — a direct descendant of Core War's cycle budget, rebuilt as a market:
  - **CPU limit:** a per-account limit on average CPU per tick, tied to subscription/game level (historically 10 CPU free/unsubscribed up to 300 at maximum progression).
  - **CPU bucket:** unused CPU accrues into a bucket capped at 10,000; bursty ticks may spend from the bucket, so the real rule is an _average_ with a bounded burst reserve — Battlecode's bytecode banking, made macroeconomic.
  - **Per-tick hard cap and isolation:** each tick's script runs in an isolated VM (isolated-vm); a script that errors or blows its heap is reset, losing in-memory state (the serialized `Memory` object, size-capped, persists; raw memory segments are separately capped).
  - Everything in the world also costs in-fiction resources (energy, minerals) with decay — structures and even rooms have upkeep, so code bloat has an in-world carrying cost too.
- **Capabilities granted via:** the `Game` API — creeps, structures, room control, market orders, inter-shard messaging — and _intents_: actions declared during the tick are collected and applied by the engine atomically after the script finishes. There is no direct I/O, no filesystem, no network; the API plus intents is the entire capability surface, and the engine can price any of it in CPU by measuring actual execution.
- **Sharing model:** library culture, fully open: code is shared as open-source colonies and npm/GitHub libraries (pathfinding and logistics libraries such as Traveler became community standards), the game supports code branches and modules designed for import, and top players publish full codebases that newcomers fork as starting colonies. Secrecy exists (private alliances' tooling) but the default current runs toward open, as in Core War.
- **Moderation model:** platform governance over a persistent world: published rules and ToS enforced by the dev team (account bans for abuse and for exploiting engine bugs), shard-level norms, and — significantly — _private servers_ with their own admins and mods, so governance is federated: the official shards are one jurisdiction among many. In-world conflict (including total colony destruction) is content, not abuse, which pushes the moderation line to out-of-game conduct and engine exploitation.
- **What broke:** the economics became the game, in both directions. CPU starvation spirals (a colony whose code turns inefficient can no longer afford the ticks to fix itself — the bucket empties, creeps starve, the player must rescue by hand-editing code under pressure) are the canonical onboarding cliff. Engine-level incidents — VM resets wiping state, shard performance degradation under load, market manipulation, alliance-scale wars fought partly through API automation (external tools polling the HTTP API softer than the intended client) — mark the boundary where "user code in a world" grows the same problems as any platform: rate limits, API abuse policy, and a permanent gap between the intended capability surface and the de-facto one (the external API). Screeps is the strongest evidence in this survey that _metering design is retention design_: the bucket (forgiveness with a cap) is widely credited with making the CPU economy playable at all.

### A7. Second Life — LSL (2003– )

Second Life is catalogued in lineage B by #5988's scope, but its acceptance criteria ask for a full fixed-field entry, so it gets one here; its lineage-B themes are expanded in the synthesis.

- **User submits:** Linden Scripting Language (LSL) scripts — event-driven, state-machine code compiled into objects ("prims" and their inventories) placed in a persistent 3D world. A script lives inside an object; objects are owned by avatars, worn, sold, and rezzed in regions run on Linden Lab's simulators (and, later, user regions under the same software).
- **Bounded by:** per-script and per-region resource bounds, hardened over two decades of griefing pressure: per-script memory (64 KB under the Mono compiler, 16 KB in the legacy VM) with the script's bytecode, heap, and stack all charged to that one budget; event-queue limits; throttles on specific expensive calls (e.g., HTTP requests, email, sensor sweeps); and region-level script-time accounting — scripts consume a share of the simulator's frame, regions report "script time," and estates can see and act on the heaviest scripts. There is no subscription CPU market as in Screeps; the bound is per-artifact caps plus _social_ enforcement against lag (see moderation).
- **Capabilities granted via:** the `ll*` function library — several hundred built-ins covering sensing (`llSensor`), physics and movement, communication (`llSay`/`llListen`, link messages between prims in an object, HTTP in/out), avatar interaction (with an explicit _permission_ handshake for sensitive acts: `llRequestPermissions` for taking controls, animating an avatar, debiting money, tracking cameras), and the later Experiences framework, which turned one-off permission grants into persistent, world-scoped capability grants a user can accept or block once.
- **Sharing model:** the canonical worked example of capability + licensing UX in a world — a per-item, per-permission matrix (**copy / modify / transfer**), set by the creator, and a _next-owner_ variant of the same permissions, so an artifact carries its reuse terms in its metadata and the platform enforces them mechanically: a no-copy object cannot be duplicated, a no-transfer object cannot be given away, a no-modify script cannot be read-edited by its buyer. Scripts themselves are routinely sold (Marketplace and in-world vendors), libraries of LSL code circulate as objects and on the LSL Wiki/Portal, and "full-perm" sales are a recognized market category with their own norms and license texts.
- **Moderation model:** layered platform governance: Linden Lab's Terms of Service and community standards enforced via an abuse-report pipeline (in-world reporting tooling, governance team actions from warnings to bans); region/estate owners hold local powers (eject, ban, return objects) making moderation federated like Screeps' private shards; and a formal DMCA process for the endemic IP disputes the permission system could not prevent (below).
- **What broke:** nearly everything, at least once, and instructively. (1) _Griefing by script:_ self-replicating objects that filled regions, physics and particle attacks, "pusher" objects — answered by per-script caps, replication limits, estate tools (object return, script-info), and region performance accounting; the arms race directly produced the modern bounds above. (2) _Permission-system circumvention:_ the "CopyBot" episode (2006) — a client that could duplicate no-copy objects by copying their rendered asset data — triggered world-wide seller protests and made the permanent lesson that _client-visible = copyable_; the permission matrix governs honest clients only, and licensing UX must be designed with that ceiling stated. (3) _Economy and policy shocks:_ in-world banks and gambling grew until policy banned them (2007–2008), and an unregulated in-world bank collapse destroyed user savings — governance arriving after the fact, at platform scale. (4) _Permission fatigue:_ the debit-permission grant was abused by scam objects, which is why sensitive capabilities moved toward explicit, revocable, scoped grants (Experiences) — the direct ancestor of the design stance #5955's capability children take.

## Lineage B — user-programmable worlds, one pass each

Each entry answers the four lineage-B themes from #5988: **resource limits** on user code, **code sharing/reuse as libraries**, **moderation of user creations**, and **asset licensing**.

### B1. MOO / LambdaMOO (1990– )

LambdaMOO (Pavel Curtis, Xerox PARC, 1990) put a full programming language (MOOcode) inside a text world and let any player create objects and program verbs on them; it is the ancestor every later system in this section cites.

- **Resource limits:** the server enforces per-task quotas — a foreground task gets a bounded tick count (and seconds) before the server suspends or kills it, with background tasks metered separately; object and disk quotas (byte-based ownership quotas) limited how much any player could create. Runaway verbs are killed by the meter, not by an admin — the earliest "fuel" system in lineage B, and the direct conceptual parent of #5962's invariants.
- **Sharing/reuse as libraries:** _inheritance as the library model_ — generic parent objects (the Generic Room, the Generic Thing, feature objects that attach whole capability bundles to a player) are specialized by child objects, so reuse is `chparent` plus verb overrides; utility packages (`$string_utils`, `$list_utils` and friends) are shared singleton objects whose verbs everyone calls. The library is a place in the world, owned and maintained by someone.
- **Moderation:** wizard authority (a small wizard class with override powers) plus the most famous governance experiment in this survey: after the 1993 "Mr. Bungle" incident (a puppet character used for virtual sexual assault, written up by Julian Dibbell) and the wizards' subsequent "New Direction" memo, LambdaMOO moved much dispute resolution to player democracy — petitions and ballots binding on wizards — reserving wizard power for technical and emergency acts. Sanctions included `@toading` (character destruction and asset seizure). The finding: _programmable worlds generate constitutional questions within a few years, whether or not the designers scheduled them._
- **Asset licensing:** informal and pre-web: ownership is tracked by the object system (every object has an owner; verb/property permission bits `r`, `w`, `x` and the `f` "fertile" flag govern reading, writing, executing, and subclassing), but there is no licensing vocabulary beyond those bits and social norms — reuse credit is reputational. The permission bits are the licensing system, exactly as in Second Life's matrix a decade later, minus commerce.

### B2. Furcadia — DragonSpeak (1996– )

Furcadia (Dragon's Eye Productions, 1996) is the deliberate low-ceiling design of lineage B: user worlds ("dreams") are uploaded maps plus **DragonSpeak** — a numbered, natural-language-ish trigger language of _cause/effect line pairs_ written for non-programmers.

- **Resource limits:** bounded by construction — DragonSpeak has no loops or recursion in its classic form; a script is a list of triggered rule blocks the server evaluates on events, so cost per event is linear in script size and the dangerous programs simply cannot be written. Dream upload size and the later PhoenixSpeak persistent-database quotas (per-character and per-dream storage caps, sold in larger tiers) bound state the way tick quotas bound compute elsewhere.
- **Sharing/reuse as libraries:** snippet culture — DragonSpeak fragments circulate on forums and in "dream weaving" tutorials and are pasted into dreams; dreams themselves are shareable artifacts (uploadable by anyone to public dream portals), and experienced weavers publish example dreams as templates. Reuse is copy-paste of text, with attribution by norm.
- **Moderation:** publisher-run with a volunteer corps (the Beekins) including a Guardian (moderator) track, plus dream-level sovereignty: a dream's owner can eject and ban visitors and control entry, so most creation-level moderation is local to the dream. Uploaded dreams sit in a shared world with content rules (rated areas, the main maps' standards) enforced by staff.
- **Asset licensing:** a marketplace before marketplaces: purchasable avatar/items ("Digos") fund the game; user art ("patches") attached to dreams is protected in practice by _dream encryption_ (uploaded dreams can be encrypted so visitors receive the experience but cannot extract the art/scripts) — a licensing mechanism implemented as access control, with the same honest-client ceiling as Second Life's permissions.

### B3. Active Worlds (1995– )

Active Worlds (1995) built its user programmability into _objects_: any placed object can carry trigger-action commands (`create`, `activate`, `bump`, `adone` triggers with actions like `move`, `rotate`, `texture`, `sign`, `url`) — a declarative mini-language attached to geometry rather than free-standing code.

- **Resource limits:** cell-based world accounting — each world cell holds a capped amount of object data, worlds have object/space budgets by license tier, and the command language is non-looping and evaluated on discrete triggers, so cost is bounded structurally like Furcadia's. Server operators set world limits; the bound is capacity planning, not per-user metering.
- **Sharing/reuse as libraries:** object yards and registries — builders copy objects from public building-supply worlds, and object _registries_ let a world reserve its custom objects so others cannot reuse the same model files; reuse happens by reference to shared object paths on object servers. "Libraries" are places (yards) and namespaces (registries), not code modules.
- **Moderation:** citizenship and world ownership — paid citizenship gates building in public worlds; world owners control their worlds' content and visitor lists; the platform's Peacekeeper volunteers patrol the public flagship world. Creation moderation is mostly _placement_ moderation (what may be built where) because the command language cannot do much harm beyond vandalism and offensive builds.
- **Asset licensing:** the registry system is the licensing mechanism — registering an object password/namespace prevents other worlds from hot-linking or reusing those assets, and commercial object/model sales operated through third-party shops; disputes were handled as registry/ownership claims by the platform. Licensing by namespace control: whoever controls the object path controls reuse.

### B4. There (2003–2010; relaunched 2011– )

There (There, Inc.) took the opposite door into lineage B: almost no user _code_, but a formal user-_creation_ pipeline — the clearest early worked example of moderation-by-review and licensing-by-contract.

- **Resource limits:** largely inapplicable to code (the world exposed little user programmability; behaviour came from the platform), and that absence is the data point: There traded the whole lineage-B code problem away and spent the effort on content instead. World-side budgets (item counts, physics) were platform-set.
- **Sharing/reuse as libraries:** reuse happened through the catalog, not code: approved creations could be bought and placed by any member, and derivative creation was constrained to the sanctioned tools (texture templates for clothing, item re-texturing within the designer program's rules).
- **Moderation:** _pre-publication review_ — creations submitted through the developer/designer program were reviewed against guidelines before they could enter the world or be sold; nothing user-made reached other users unreviewed. In-world behaviour moderation was conventional staff/community moderation. The contrast with LambdaMOO and Second Life (publish first, govern after) is deliberate and is the governance child's cleanest comparison case.
- **Asset licensing:** contractual — designer agreements assigned the platform the rights it needed, warranted the creator's IP, and paid creators a share of sales in the in-world currency (Therebucks, convertible at a published rate at the time). Licensing here is a _business contract with a revenue share_, not a permission matrix — the model Roblox later industrialized.

### B5. Roblox (2006– )

Roblox industrialized lineage B: every experience is user-built, scripted in sandboxed Lua (now Luau), on platform servers, for an audience that arrived in the hundreds of millions.

- **Resource limits:** layered platform budgets — per-experience server memory and script performance limits (scripts that overrun are throttled/killed; the engine exposes per-script performance stats to creators), replication and physics budgets, and explicit _service quotas_ for persistence (DataStore request budgets scale with player count; exceeding them throttles saves — a Screeps-like average-with-burst budget expressed as requests/minute). Client/server split is itself a limit: client scripts see only replicated state, and the post-2014 FilteringEnabled default made server authority the enforced norm after an era of client-trust exploits.
- **Sharing/reuse as libraries:** the **Toolbox** — a built-in marketplace of free models, scripts, and plugins, insertable in one click — plus ModuleScripts for in-experience libraries and a large open-source ecosystem (community libraries and frameworks published on GitHub). The Toolbox is LambdaMOO's generic-object library at platform scale, including its failure mode (next bullet).
- **Moderation:** the largest moderation operation in this survey: every uploaded asset passes automated (and sampled human) moderation before it is usable, experiences are reviewed against policy, chat is filtered, and enforcement runs from asset deletion to account termination at industrial scale. The free-model ecosystem produced the canonical _library-supply-chain_ incident class: popular free models containing hidden malicious scripts ("backdoored models") that compromised every experience that inserted them — answered with creator education, script-visibility tooling in Studio, and community auditing norms rather than closing the Toolbox.
- **Asset licensing:** platform terms as the licensing layer — creators grant the platform broad rights, keep ownership of their IP in principle, can sell assets and experiences for Robux convertible to cash via DevEx (with eligibility thresholds), and UGC commerce (limited items, resale) runs on platform-set rules with platform-set revenue splits. DMCA processing is industrialized. Licensing disputes are resolved as platform-policy matters, the end state of the arc that began with LambdaMOO's permission bits.

## Cross-cutting findings

1. **Every durable system meters something, and the meter becomes the game.** Core War cycles → Battlecode bytecodes → Screeps CPU+bucket → Roblox DataStore budgets → LambdaMOO task ticks. Meters that only cap (hard per-tick ceilings) produce cliff-edge failures (Screeps' starvation spiral, Robocode's skipped turns); meters that cap an _average with a bounded burst reserve_ (Screeps bucket, Battlecode banking, Roblox per-minute budgets) are consistently the survivable designs. (#5962's invariants should be stated as averages with burst reserves, not single-tick walls, wherever the underlying resource allows it.)
2. **The capability surface is always a library, and its shape is the security model.** Intrinsic functions (CROBOTS), `ll*` (LSL), the `Game` API + intents (Screeps), node vocabularies (Gladiabots), DragonSpeak lines (Furcadia), object triggers (Active Worlds): in every case, what user code can do is exactly what a named, enumerable function set allows, and every historical escape came from the seams (external APIs in Screeps, client trust in early Roblox, rendered-asset copying in Second Life) rather than through the library itself.
3. **Sharing defaults decide the ecosystem.** Open-by-default lineages (Core War, Screeps, LambdaMOO inheritance, Roblox Toolbox) produced teaching cultures and library ecosystems _and_ supply-chain risk (backdoored free models, evolver opacity). Closed-by-default pockets (RoboWar's locked robots, Furcadia's encrypted dreams, Active Worlds' registries) protected creators and measurably slowed collective learning. Second Life's copy/modify/transfer matrix is the only system here that made the choice **per artifact, at publish time, in the artifact's own metadata** — and it is the model with a commerce ecosystem to show for it.
4. **Moderation arrives in one of three shapes, and the shape is chosen by persistence.** Hermetic, stateless competition (all of lineage A except Screeps) needs only tournament administration. Persistent worlds choose between publish-first-govern-after (LambdaMOO, Second Life, Roblox — with governance crises as the tuition) and review-before-publish (There — with throughput and creator-friction as the tuition). There is no fourth option on record; a #5955 governance design is choosing a point on this triangle, not escaping it.
5. **Licensing is enforced at exactly the layer the platform controls, and honest about its ceiling everywhere else.** Permission bits (LambdaMOO), permission matrix (Second Life), namespace/registry control (Active Worlds), access control/encryption (Furcadia), contract + revenue share (There, Roblox). CopyBot's lesson generalizes: anything rendered to a client can be copied by that client; licensing systems that state this ceiling (and put their real enforcement on distribution and commerce, as Second Life's Marketplace and Roblox's catalog do) age better than systems that promise technical prevention.
6. **Tiered submission widens the funnel without splitting the world.** Bot Land's none/Blockly/text-subset tiers and Gladiabots' pure-visual tier show non-programmers participating in a code-shaped game over the _same_ capability surface as programmers. The cost is parity drift between tiers (Bot Land concedes its tiers only aspire to parity) — tiers must compile to one artifact/semantics, or the tiers become different games.

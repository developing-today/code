## 1. Principles

1. **Extensibility is the architecture.** Primitives/adapters/hooks are designed for the full matrix of stores, buckets, streams, transports. Only *implementing* the long tail is de-prioritized (§0).
2. **Dual tier-1.** Rust binaries and the Cloudflare/Turso hosted edition are both fully featured primaries; divergence is deliberate and documented, never a reduced port.
3. **Signed or it didn't happen.** Data is accepted only when signed and validated, regardless of source (§1) — and this gate is a **primitive invariant**: configuration cannot disable it (the §1/§7 tension, ruled here and recorded for the summit).
4. **Our server is a peer.** No protocol special-casing for our own nodes; every capability we use is a config option anyone could replicate.
5. **Mechanism in primitives, behavior in configuration** (§7) — subject to principle 3.
6. **Content is addressed once, located many times.** Bytes are content-addressed and immutable; locations, names, and attributes are separate, mutable, signed objects around them (§3–§5).

### §1 — Peer trust + federation (120)

**A. Peer classes (S1.1–S1.11).** S1.1 Every peer belongs to an explicit peer class; no unclassed peer. · S1.2 Class: fully trusted. · S1.3 Class: send-only (we send, we do not accept). · S1.4 Class: accept-signed-from. · S1.5 (implied) Classification is directional, not symmetric. · S1.6 (implied) Classification is per-peer, not global. · S1.7 (implied) Class governs acceptance behaviour; it does not by itself confer cryptographic trust. · S1.8 (implied) Class is changeable over time (promote/demote/revoke) without redesign. · S1.9 (implied) Default class for an unknown peer is the most restrictive: no acceptance. · S1.10 (implied) Send-only is a legitimate permanent posture, not a degraded state. · S1.11 (implied) Fully trusted does NOT mean exempt from validation.

**B. The signature gate (S1.12–S1.25).** S1.12 Data is accepted ONLY when cryptographically signed AND validated — every source, no exceptions by source. · S1.13 Unsigned data is not accepted, even from a fully trusted peer. · S1.14 Invalidly signed data is not accepted, even from a fully trusted peer. · S1.15 (implied) Internal peers are under the same gate as external. · S1.16 (implied) Local/loopback/in-process channels are under the same gate — no channel exception. · S1.17 (implied) Validation is distinct from signature presence (a non-verifying or misattributed signature fails). · S1.18 (implied) Validation binds data to a signer identity, not merely to "some valid signature". · S1.19 (implied) Acceptance is a gate at ingest, not post-hoc cleanup. · S1.20 (implied) The gate is a mechanism/invariant, not a behaviour configuration may switch off (tension with §7 — ruled in principle 3). · S1.21 (implied) Key rotation must not retroactively invalidate data validly signed under a then-current key. · S1.22 (implied) Key revocation/compromise has a defined effect on previously accepted data (revalidate/flag/quarantine). · S1.23 (implied) Replay/freshness protection is required: re-presented old signed data is handled by a specified rule. · S1.24 (implied) Signature-suite agility: deprecating an algorithm must not break the protocol. · S1.25 (implied) Rejection under the gate is an observable, recordable event (silent drops would defeat reputation and inconsistency detection).

**C. Implicit-trust sync (S1.26–S1.33).** S1.26 Internal peers carry implicit trust — a posture distinct from external federation peers. · S1.27 Implicit-trust peers sync toward 1:1 sameness. · S1.28 Sameness is qualified "within reason" — a convergence target, not an absolute invariant. · S1.29 (implied) "Within reason" includes storage capacity, retention policy, configured scope. · S1.30 (implied) "Within reason" includes tenant/trust-domain boundaries — sameness never crosses a boundary data must not cross (see #9083). · S1.31 (implied) Sync is convergence over time; temporary divergence is normal. · S1.32 (implied) Divergence is detectable (otherwise "sync toward" is unverifiable). · S1.33 (implied) Anti-entropy/reconciliation is the implied mechanism class; specifics deferred to the CRDT exploration.

**D. Conflicts first; CRDT later (S1.34–S1.46).** S1.34 A sync conflict's first output is a conflict report. · S1.35 Reports come before any automatic merge — no silent auto-resolution precedes the report. · S1.36 (implied) A conflict report is a first-class artifact (addressable, retainable, actionable), not a log line. · S1.37 (implied) A report identifies the conflicting versions, signers/sources, and the data in dispute. · S1.38 (implied) Unresolved conflicts do not block unrelated sync. · S1.39 CRDT/merge exploration is explicitly later — a research track, not a dependency of the first federation design. · S1.40 (implied) Conflict-report-first remains available even if a merge mechanism is later adopted. · S1.41 Research: evaluate **Automerge** (data-model fit, fleet-scale performance, signed-history compatibility, interop cost). · S1.42 Research: identify what "diamonds" refers to (believed Diamond Types) and evaluate on the same criteria — the identification is part of the research. · S1.43 Research: evaluate the **Willow protocol** (reconciliation efficiency, namespace/subspace model vs our scoping, signed-only acceptance fit). · S1.44 Research: evaluate **OCAPN** (capability authority model vs the peer-class model; can attenuation express send-only / accept-signed-from natively?). · S1.45 (implied) The four explorations are a named set evaluated on a common criteria matrix. · S1.46 (implied) The set is not exhaustive — other prior art found is captured, not excluded.

**E. Federated query fan-out (S1.47–S1.63).** S1.47 A federated query checks the server's own store first. · S1.48 Then it fans out to remotes. · S1.49 Fan-out targets a prioritized list of remotes. · S1.50 Those remotes are known not to hold everything. · S1.51 (implied) A remote's negative answer is not a global negative. · S1.52 (implied) Prioritization is explicit, by stated criteria. · S1.53 (implied) Prioritization inputs (trust class, reputation, coverage, latency, cost) are design decisions, candidates for configuration. · S1.54 A trusted remote may re-fan-out to its remotes — recursion. · S1.55 (implied) Re-fan-out is restricted to trusted remotes; relay authority is not granted to accept-only peers. · S1.56 Recursion is bounded by max-hops. · S1.57 (implied) Max-hops is a stated bound carried by every query (decremented/tracked per relay). · S1.58 Signatures and validation apply throughout: query, relayed copies, responses. · S1.59 (implied) A relayed response preserves the original responder's signature end-to-end — no hop-by-hop authority substitution. · S1.60 (implied) Relayed responses are validated to the direct-response standard. · S1.61 (implied) Fan-out must not amplify without bound; breadth × depth is a resource surface max-hops alone doesn't bound. · S1.62 (implied) A query carries a stable request identity across all copies/relays (prerequisite for duplicate handling). · S1.63 (implied) Fan-out leaks query interest to every remote queried — a named privacy surface, accepted or mitigated explicitly.

**E′. Duplicate delivery (S1.64–S1.72).** S1.64 The same request can legitimately arrive more than once (direct + via relay) — normal, not an error. · S1.65 All four duplicate behaviours are specced: inform users, short-circuit, dedup, loop-inform. · S1.66 Inform: a duplicate is surfaced to the requester, not silently swallowed. · S1.67 Short-circuit: a duplicate of an in-flight/answered request is not re-executed from scratch. · S1.68 Dedup: duplicate work/results are deduplicated into one coherent result set. · S1.69 Loop-inform: duplication evidencing a loop is reported specifically as a loop. · S1.70 (implied) Duplicate-vs-loop discrimination needs request identity + hop/path information. · S1.71 (implied) Dedup is by request identity, not query text — the same query asked later is a new request. · S1.72 (implied) Dedup state is finite; its retention window and expiry behaviour are specified.

**E″. Routing state (S1.73–S1.78).** S1.73 Servers may store routing state. · S1.74 Servers periodically validate their known network shape against reality. · S1.75 (implied) Routing state goes stale; staleness is expected and handled. · S1.76 (implied) Validation detects at least vanished peers, appeared peers, topology changes affecting fan-out. · S1.77 (implied) Routing state is an attack surface (poisoning redirects fan-out, suppresses results, manufactures loops) — routing updates fall under the same signature/validation discipline as data. · S1.78 (implied) A server's known shape is partial by design; no complete topology is assumed.

**F. Reputation (S1.79–S1.91).** S1.79 A reputation system exists: peers carry ratings. · S1.80 (implied) Ratings derive from evidence, not assertion alone. · S1.81 (implied) Reputation feeds behaviour — at minimum fan-out prioritization and peer-class changes. · S1.82 Trust adapts from evidence automatically per stated rules (not only manual reclassification). · S1.83 Takeover case (explicit): a highly trusted server turns malicious (takeover or key compromise); the system must survive it. · S1.84 (implied) Trust must be revocable at speed — slow adaptation is not a defence. · S1.85 (implied) Blast-radius limit: data accepted from a taken-over server before detection is identifiable as a set. · S1.86 (implied) A defined post-detection posture for that server's prior data (revalidate/flag/quarantine/repudiate) is chosen and specced. · S1.87 (implied) Takeover vs honest key compromise are distinguished, or explicitly conflated — recovery differs. · S1.88 Reputation includes protections against bad actors abusing the reputation system itself. · S1.89 (implied) Protections cover rating manipulation: ballot-stuffing/self-promotion, retaliatory down-rating, Sybil raters — a stated position per failure mode. · S1.90 (implied) Protections cover whitewashing (fresh identity resetting reputation) — in tension with open federation, recorded as a tension. · S1.91 (implied) Protections cover collusion — load-bearing, because collusion is the defeat condition of inconsistency detection and cannot be assumed away.

**G. Public claims/dispute log (S1.92–S1.99).** S1.92 A public log for claims and disputes about federation conduct exists. · S1.93 Entry form: "server X claims Y was done incorrectly" — a claim by an identified server about another's act. · S1.94 (implied) Entries are claims, not verdicts. · S1.95 (implied) The accused party can answer in the same log (right of reply). · S1.96 (implied) Entries are attributable and non-repudiable — signed by the claimant. · S1.97 (implied) The log is append-only in effect; corrections are new entries. · S1.98 (implied) The log is evidence input to reputation. · S1.99 (implied) Frivolous/spam claims are themselves a bad-actor case needing protection (no cost-free smear/flood channel).

**H. Acknowledgment-based inconsistency detection (S1.100–S1.113).** S1.100 Inconsistency is detectable from acknowledgments: what a server acknowledged receiving is evidence against its later claims. · S1.101 Worked example (explicit): A says "don't have it"; B produces a recipient list including A — contradiction. · S1.102 (implied) Detection is by a third party comparing denial vs recipient list; it does not require A's cooperation. · S1.103 (implied) Acks/recipient lists are retained and producible as evidence. · S1.104 (implied) Acks are signed/non-repudiable to serve as evidence. · S1.105 Defeating detection requires collusion (e.g. B withholds/falsifies the recipient list). · S1.106 (implied) Collusion is the residual, accepted-by-analysis risk — detection raises the cost of lying from one server to a colluding set; it does not make lying impossible. · S1.107 (implied) The size/composition of the colluding set needed is a security parameter worth stating. · S1.108 (implied) A detected contradiction belongs in the public log and in reputation evidence. · S1.109 Threat case: tampering with (federated) search results is a named threat. · S1.110 (implied) Tampering decomposes into omission, injection, reordering/manipulation, staleness. · S1.111 (implied) Injection is bounded by the signature gate (an injected result can't carry its claimed source's valid signature) — omission is the hard case, leaving no cryptographic trace. · S1.112 (implied) Omission detection depends on cross-checking and acknowledgments — the design states which countermeasures it uses. · S1.113 (implied) A remote claiming completeness for a scope can be caught omitting; whether remotes make completeness claims is a recorded design decision.

**I. Non-specialness (S1.114–S1.120).** S1.114 Our system is not special. · S1.115 Every capability is a configuration option anyone could replicate; none is reserved to our implementation by the protocol. · S1.116 (implied) No privileged protocol position for our servers (same classes, reputation, log exposure). · S1.117 (implied) No hardcoded self-preference (no default trust/priority exemption for our nodes that a replicator couldn't also configure). · S1.118 (implied) Non-specialness is testable as interop: an equivalently configured independent implementation participates equivalently. · S1.119 (implied) First-party/hosted deployments are instances of configurations, not a distinct kind of system (link: #9078). · S1.120 (implied) We cannot be the unaccountable operator/arbiter of the reputation or log systems — that would be a special position.

### §4 — Namespaces (93; child-inventory numbering 39–131, renumbered here S4.1–S4.93 in the same order)

**General model (S4.1–S4.18).** S4.1 A namespace is a named addressing space with its own rules, distinct from its host server. · S4.2 A server hosts multiple namespaces. · S4.3 Each server has a namespace registry. · S4.4 The registry is definable per server. · S4.5 Different servers' registries may diverge — by design. · S4.6 (implied) No global namespace registry and no globally canonical resolution exists. · S4.7 (implied) The same name may resolve differently on different servers without either being in error. · S4.8 Each namespace can have its own URL handler. · S4.9 Each namespace can have its own encoding (e.g. base64). · S4.10 (implied) Encoding is per-namespace, not a global URL property. · S4.11 An `id://` URL design covers these namespaces. · S4.12 It must follow the URI specifications. · S4.13 It must be human-readable. · S4.14 It must be expandable without breaking existing URLs. · S4.15 Drew's `idb64+...` sketch is NOT adopted — it is a sketch to evaluate/replace. · S4.16 (implied) Human-readability rules out a common case that is only an opaque base64 blob. · S4.17 (implied) URI compliance constrains scheme, authority/path, reserved characters, percent-encoding, case/normalisation, length — all to be settled in the design. · S4.18 (implied) A namespace definition declares at least: core type, handler, encoding, resolution/write rules.

**The two cores (S4.19–S4.24).** S4.19 Exactly two core namespaces exist, from which families derive. · S4.20 Core (a) is the content-addressed namespace. · S4.21 Core (b) is the array-address namespace. · S4.22 (implied) Derived families are configurations/compositions of a core, not new storage primitives, unless a design proves otherwise. · S4.23 In core (b), an address is an array of byte-strings. · S4.24 Each array item is either null or bytes.

**Array-address mechanics (S4.25–S4.44).** S4.25 (implied) Item order is significant — position is part of the address. · S4.26 Address length is arbitrary, 0..n. · S4.27 The zero-length address is valid. · S4.28 (implied) Null items are meaningful sparse slots, not merely absent values. · S4.29 A bare string query is shorthand for an array address. · S4.30 A bare string resolves via a server-configurable default index. · S4.31 Drew's example configuration is index 2 with [0],[1] null. · S4.32 S4.31 is one server's configuration, NOT the rule — never hardcoded. · S4.33 (implied) Different default-index configs resolve the same bare string to different addresses. · S4.34 Values are stored as TYPE + BYTES (type tag + payload). · S4.35 Equality is type-sensitive: number 1 ≠ string "1". · S4.36 (implied) Identical bytes with different type tags are different values. · S4.37 Serialization is type-aware. · S4.38 (implied) Serialization round-trips: a value reads back with the type it was written with. · S4.39 Arbitrary objects serialize into this namespace. · S4.40 (implied) The object serialization format, and which types are primitive vs encoded objects, must be designed. · S4.41 Interfaces may stream results. · S4.42 Interfaces accept a max-items parameter. · S4.43 (implied) Streaming + max-items require defined truncation/continuation semantics. · S4.44 (implied) Max-items is an interface capability, not a limit on address length or stored items.

**String-path names (S4.45–S4.63).** S4.45 A derived string-path namespace of human, Wikipedia-style names exists. · S4.46 Example: `us president.md` (spaces, extension included). · S4.47 A name resolves to a target, per-server. · S4.48 Resolution may be hand-picked by the admin. · S4.49 Resolution may be picked algorithmically. · S4.50 (implied) Both modes must be representable per server. · S4.51 (implied) Names are expected to be ambiguous/colliding; multiple candidate resolutions exist. · S4.52 Search shows the resolutions (plural), not a hidden winner. · S4.53 Resolutions are lens-able. · S4.54 Lens 1: own server's overrides. · S4.55 Lens 2: popularity by total count. · S4.56 Lens 3: popularity among users you have interacted with. · S4.57 Lens 4: popularity among users of a given standing. · S4.58 (implied) "Standing" must be defined (what it is, who assigns it, how it's queried). · S4.59 Authority groups curate names/resolutions. · S4.60 A group is adoptable as a preferred filter/lens. · S4.61 Example: a "wiki group" curating Wikipedia-style names. · S4.62 (implied) Adopting a group is voluntary — a preference, not a global authority. · S4.63 (implied) Multiple groups can curate overlapping names; a viewer can switch preferred group.

**Numeric registries (S4.64–S4.73).** S4.64 A derived numeric-registry family exists (addresses/values are numbers). · S4.65 Integers supported. · S4.66 Floats supported. · S4.67 Decimals supported. · S4.68 Bigints supported. · S4.69 Numbers are infinitely growable (no fixed max magnitude/precision). · S4.70 (implied) That requires an extensible byte encoding, not fixed-width fields. · S4.71 A numeric registry may be the byte system plus numeric-style write validation. · S4.72 (implied) Numeric-ness is then enforced at write by validation, not a separate engine. · S4.73 (implied) Validation defines canonical encoding, ordering, precision, and rejection rules per numeric kind.

**Streams (S4.74–S4.82).** S4.74 A derived stream namespace family exists. · S4.75 Text streams. · S4.76 Chat-as-typed in multi-user documents (contributions addressable while produced). · S4.77 Voice in progress. · S4.78 Video in progress. · S4.79 (implied) A stream is addressable before it finishes; in-progress state is first-class. · S4.80 A finished stream redirects to the final stored object. · S4.81 (implied) The redirect is namespace semantics: following a completed stream's address yields the stored object. · S4.82 (implied) Stream lifecycle and write-vs-subscribe rights must be designed.

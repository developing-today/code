# Design: multi-device sync cluster — knowledge layer (local-first sync, conflicts, offline, E2EE, attachments)

  - One `/id-sync/1` handshake, range flights on `/id-range-sync/1` (`doc/2026-10-29T16-21-00Z_design_id_sync_alpn_split/`), range wired under the handshake via the per-call lock store seam (`doc/2026-10-29T16-57-03Z_design_id_sync_range_store_seam/`).

## Decision to make

For the #2048 knowledge layer used from several of a person's devices: local-first or cloud-first sync, how conflicts resolve, what offline editing means, what end-to-end encryption is promised, and how attachments/media move — reconciled with ID's existing sync designs rather than inventing a second sync system for notes.

## Recommendation

**Local-first, on the existing ID sync substrate. There is no knowledge-layer sync protocol; there is the knowledge layer's *policy* over ID sync.** Concretely:

1. **Local-first (§1).** Every device keeps a full local replica of the knowledge objects it is authorised to read, edits are signed operations appended locally, and sync is replication of operation logs and blobs between devices and relays. Servers/relays are availability infrastructure, never an authority and never the only copy.
2. **Conflicts are revision forks, resolved in the open (§2).** Concurrent edits produce sibling revisions in the revision DAG — both signed, both kept. Claims-type data (tags, names, memberships) converges by its observed-remove semantics; body conflicts surface as forks with a deterministic default head and an explicit merge revision as the resolution act. Nothing is silently overwritten; there is no whole-object last-writer-wins.
3. **Offline is the normal case (§3).** Full read/write offline over the replicated scope; operations queue in the local log and replicate on reconnect by the substrate's existing log/range method selection. Offline is not a degraded mode with its own rules.
4. **E2EE is tiered by the read-authority ruling (§4).** Objects whose resolved read authority is public sync signed-but-cleartext (they are world-readable by ruling; encryption would buy nothing and would break public indexing). Objects with a narrower resolved read authority sync encrypted under group keys via p2panda-encryption; relays store and forward ciphertext and never hold keys.
5. **Attachments are blobs, synced lazily (§5).** Content-addressed blobs referenced by revisions, transferred separately from log sync through the blob gate: small blobs eager, large media on demand with placeholders, hash-verified on arrival, relay-hosted for availability.

## §1 Local-first vs cloud-first

**Decision: local-first.**

- A device holds the complete operation log and blob set for its sync scope (§3) locally. Reads never wait on a network. Writes are local signed operations (`note.create`, `note.revise`, claims, tombstones) appended to the local log first; replication is subsequent and asynchronous.
- This is the only shape compatible with what already exists: #2048 revisions are *signed by the writer's key* — a cloud-first design would need the server to countersign or order operations, adding an authority the object model deliberately does not have; and ID's sync substrate is peer/relay replication of logs (p2panda-sync, range sync), not client–server RPC against a canonical store.
- It is also the competitor baseline the parent programme measured this cluster against: the sticky knowledge tools (Obsidian, Logseq) are local-first, and the programme's own cluster statement poses the question as local-first *vs* cloud-first precisely because users' offline and ownership expectations are set there.
- Relays (and any hosted node) keep replicas to provide availability when all of a person's devices are offline, and to serve public-read content. A relay replica has no ordering or acceptance authority: an operation is valid because it is signed and well-formed, not because a relay accepted it.

**Rejected: cloud-first (server-canonical store, devices as caches).** It would (a) fork ID into two sync architectures — worlds/directory on replication, knowledge on client–server — for no user-visible gain; (b) make offline editing an exception path bolted onto a server-ordered model, instead of the natural consequence of local logs; (c) put a server's availability and honesty inside the trust boundary for a person's own notes, against the signed-operation model's grain. Revisit only if a future requirement needs server-ordered knowledge operations (e.g. server-enforced global uniqueness for some knowledge object type); none exists in the programme.

## §2 Conflict resolution

Conflicts are defined per data shape, because #2048 has three shapes with different honest answers:

- **Claims (tags, names/aliases, memberships, to-do state): no conflict surface.** Tag claims are already observed-remove sets; concurrent add/remove resolves by the claim semantics #2048 landed. Two devices' claims union deterministically. This design adds nothing.
- **Revisions (note bodies): fork, don't merge silently.** If two devices revise the same parent revision concurrently, both children exist; the note has two heads. The *view projection* (writer-set view projection design, linked above) presents one default head by a deterministic rule — highest (Lamport clock, author public key) — so every device converges on the same displayed head without communication. The non-default head is never deleted or hidden: the UI must be able to show "also edited on another device", and resolution is an ordinary `note.revise` merge revision naming **both** heads as parents, after which the note has one head again. A merge revision may be authored by a person or, for purely additive non-overlapping text regions, proposed by a tool — but it is a signed revision like any other, so auto-merge is auditable and reversible.
- **Tombstones vs concurrent edits:** a tombstone removes a note from the view; a concurrent revision of a tombstoned note does not resurrect it silently. The tombstone wins for visibility (deletion is the safety-relevant direction), the concurrent revision remains in the log and is visible in history, and un-deletion is an explicit signed act. This matches #2048's tombstone-removal model; it is stated here because sync is where the race actually occurs.

**Rejected: whole-object last-writer-wins.** It discards a signed revision's content from the view with no trace in the object itself — data loss as a *policy*, and un-auditable in a signed model whose point is provenance. **Rejected for v1: automatic CRDT text merge of bodies.** Bodies are content blobs behind immutable revisions in #2048; character-level merge would need a second content representation. Fork + merge revision covers the actual multi-device case (usually one person, occasionally editing on two devices) with machinery the model already has. If simultaneous co-editing of one note by several people becomes a requirement, that is a live-collaboration design, not a sync change.

## §3 Offline editing and sync scope

- **Offline editing is unrestricted within scope:** create, revise, tag, rename, delete (tombstone), and organise all work offline and produce the same signed operations they would online. On reconnect, log sync (or range sync when the peer is far behind, per the substrate's method selection) replicates the missing operations both ways; the view converges by §2.
- **Sync scope** is per device and explicit: the default scope is every knowledge object the device's identity may read (its own plus group-shared plus public objects it has opened or subscribed to). Public-read objects are not force-replicated to every device merely because they are readable — read authority (§4) is not a replication mandate. Scope changes are local policy, not protocol events.
- **Ordering truth:** there is no global wall-clock ordering and none is needed; causal order is the revision DAG plus per-author Lamport clocks, which the substrate already carries. "Last edited" displays use (Lamport, author) order and must be labelled as device-claimed time where a timestamp is shown, since offline devices' clocks are untrusted.
- **Storage pressure:** a device may prune blobs (§5) but not log operations for notes in scope; pruning history is out of scope for this design and would need its own decision record (it interacts with range sync and with peers that are far behind).

## §4 End-to-end encryption expectations

The parent's read-authority ruling (§header) divides the knowledge layer into two confidentiality classes, and sync honours exactly that division:

- **Public class** — resolved read authority is *public*: operations and blobs sync **signed, integrity-protected, and cleartext**. Promising encryption here would be false advertising (anyone may read anyway) and would break the public goods the ruling enables (public indexing, link previews, serving by any relay). The promise for this class is *authenticity and integrity*, not confidentiality: every operation is signed, every blob hash-verified.
- **Restricted class** — resolved read authority is narrower than public (specific groups/users): operations' payloads and blobs sync **end-to-end encrypted under the read group's key(s)**, using vendored p2panda-encryption. Relays and non-member devices may store/forward ciphertext but hold no keys; a device without the group key cannot read content even with a complete replica — that, and nothing weaker, is what "end-to-end encrypted" means in this design.
- **Stated honestly — metadata:** relays necessarily observe sync topics, timing, and ciphertext sizes, and the handshake reveals which log a device asks about. This design does not promise metadata privacy against relay operators for v1; claiming otherwise would be the kind of overpromise the expectations question exists to prevent. Reducing topic metadata (e.g. opaque topic identifiers per group) is compatible future work and does not change the object model.
- **Key events are operations:** group key rotation and membership change follow the group `Read`/`Write` authority model of #2048 and the directory/group designs; a removed reader keeps access to ciphertext they already decrypted (unavoidable, stated plainly) and receives no new keys, so post-removal operations are confidential from them.
- **Compliance with the ruling:** encryption never *narrows* access below the resolved read authority (a public object stays publicly readable — §4 public class), and never *widens* it (sync, relays, and AI/indexing surfaces must not expose restricted content to anyone the resolution order would deny — the parent ruling's AI clause applies to sync-side indexes identically).

## §5 Attachment and media sync

- Attachments and media are **content blobs** referenced from revision content, exactly as #2048 models them and as the importer designs under the parent programme already assume (e.g. resources → blobs in the Joplin design). Sync of blobs is a separate flow from log sync: log operations replicate first (they are small and make the graph coherent), blobs follow through the substrate's **blob gate** (Phase 3 P3.4).
- **Eager vs lazy:** blobs below a size threshold (default inherited from the blob gate's configuration, not re-decided here) replicate eagerly with the log; larger media replicate **on demand** — the referencing revision renders a placeholder carrying the blob hash, size, and media type, and opening the attachment fetches the blob from any peer or relay that has it, hash-verified on arrival. A device may pin blobs for offline use; pinning is local policy.
- **Availability:** relays may host blobs (including encrypted blobs for the restricted class — a relay hosting ciphertext learns nothing beyond §4's metadata). Deleting a blob everywhere follows the tombstone path: the referencing claim is removed, and blob garbage collection is a separate, later decision (it must not race a far-behind peer that still needs the blob — same caution as §3 history pruning).
- **Dependency, named not duplicated:** wiring the blob gate into `serve` was left open by Phase 3 (P3.4 "gate not wired into serve: open issue"). Knowledge-layer media sync depends on that wiring; this design does not re-plan it. Until it lands, the interim behaviour is eager-only blob transfer over the log-sync connection for in-scope blobs, with the size threshold set conservatively.

## §7 Parent-decision compliance and divergences

- **Read-authority ruling (#5678): complied with, and load-bearing.** The public/restricted split in §4 is derived from the ruling's resolution order (object → user default → server config → public); sync evaluates the same resolution for every object it replicates or encrypts. No divergence.
- **#2048 object model: consumed as-is.** Fork/merge (§2), claims convergence, tombstones, and group Read/Write authority are the model's own semantics applied to the sync setting. No divergence.
- **ID sync substrate: consumed, not forked.** This design adds *policy* (scope, tiers, thresholds, presentation) over log/range sync, relays, and the blob gate; it specifies no new wire protocol. The one dependency gap (blob gate into `serve`) is flagged in §5 rather than papered over with a knowledge-layer side channel. No divergence.
- **Programme method (#5678 step 4 — decide per cluster: adopt / adapt / reject):** this cluster **adapts** the mature tools' local-first sync to ID's multiplayer object model; cloud-first sync as practised by hosted tools (Notion/Evernote) is **rejected with reasons** in §1, and per-tool sync *emulation* (e.g. syncing against a tool's own cloud) is out of scope here — that question belongs to the individual importer designs (cf. the Joplin design's split on continuous sync vs one-shot import).

## §8 Invariants implementation must hold

1. An operation is accepted into a replica iff it is signed, well-formed, and its author held `Write` authority for the object at its causal position — relay acceptance is never the criterion.
2. No sync path silently discards a signed revision: every head of every note is retrievable from the log until an explicit, signed merge or tombstone-related act addresses it, and even then it remains in history.
3. All devices that have replicated the same operation set present the same default head per note (deterministic projection, §2).
4. A restricted-class object's plaintext is never present on a device or relay that lacks the read group's key; public-class objects are never key-gated.
5. Sync never exposes content to a reader the read-authority resolution order would deny — including via indexes, previews, and placeholders (§5 placeholders carry hash/size/type only, never titles or thumbnails of restricted media).
6. Offline operations are indistinguishable in form and validity from online ones; reconnect convergence needs no special "offline conflict" path beyond §2.
7. Blobs are content-addressed and verified on arrival from any source, peer or relay.

## §9 What this design does not do

- No implementation, and no change to the sync wire protocols (`/id-sync/1`, `/id-range-sync/1`).
- No live multi-author co-editing (presence, cursors, character-level merge) — §2 names the trigger that would open that design.
- No history pruning / log compaction policy (§3) and no blob garbage-collection policy (§5); both are follow-on decisions if storage pressure makes them real.
- No per-tool cloud sync emulation (see §7, programme method).
